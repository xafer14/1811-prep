## Summary
- A small free site is probably NOT a CCPA "business" today (thresholds: >$26.625M revenue, or 100,000+ CA consumers/households, or 50%+ revenue from selling PI), but traffic growth or ad/analytics "sharing" could change that. Still publish a privacy policy: CalOPPA-style disclosure applies to any site collecting PII from CA residents (primary text not fetched; 403).
- Some state laws (Texas, Nebraska) key off SBA "small business" status, not user counts; a micro operator is likely exempt from most duties, but sensitive-data sale still needs consent in Texas (secondary source).
- CAN-SPAM: needs a valid postal address; a registered PO box or registered private mailbox (CMRA) qualifies, so an overseas solo operator can use a US mail-forwarding/CMRA address. Honor opt-outs within 10 business days; penalty up to $53,088 per email.
- Use double opt-in for the newsletter: not required by CAN-SPAM, but it is best practice and helps with consent proof and deliverability.
- COPPA applies to child-directed sites or actual knowledge of under-13 users; the audience is adults, so state "not for under-13 / under-18" and do not collect age-targeted data. (superseded 2026-11-24, see Addendum: 17-year-old military candidates are in scope, so "not for under-18" is wrong)
- Federal affiliation risk is the top legal risk: 18 U.S.C. 701 (badges/insignia), 709 (FBI/Secret Service names implying approval), 712 (private investigation/collection services), plus the FTC Impersonation Rule (16 CFR 461). Do not use any agency seal, badge, or look-alike; use clear non-affiliation disclaimers; avoid agency-lookalike domains.
- 17 U.S.C. 105 means federal-employee works are public domain in the US, but this does not cover seals/trademarks, third-party content, or foreign-law protection. Official hiring test content is confidential (FBI applicants sign a nondisclosure form); write original practice content only.
- Turkey: KVKK clearly applies to a Turkey-established controller; its reach to data of US-only users is unsettled. GDPR likely does not apply if the site does not target the EU. Treat KVKK as applicable to be safe (VERBIS/cross-border rules need local counsel).
- ADA Title III: DOJ says web content of private businesses must be accessible but sets no mandated technical standard; WCAG 2.x AA is the practical target.
- A fitness/PFT section needs a health disclaimer and no individualized medical advice.

## Findings

### 1. US privacy
**CCPA/CPRA thresholds.** A for-profit doing business in CA is covered if it meets any one: gross annual revenue over $25M (statutory), buys/sells/shares PI of 100,000+ CA residents or households, or gets 50%+ of annual revenue from selling CA residents' PI (https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa, accessed 2026-11-24). The revenue figure is CPI-adjusted to $26,625,000 effective 2025-01-01 (https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html, 2026-11-24); a later adjustment for 2027 is (unverified). CPPA lists regulations effective 2026-01-01 incl. risk assessment/cybersecurity audit/ADMT rules (https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/, 2026-11-24). Takeaway: a free site with no revenue is unlikely covered unless it reaches 100,000 CA consumers/households AND "buys, sells or shares" data (e.g., cross-context behavioral ads). Using ad pixels or sharing emails with ad networks would increase exposure. Whether plain first-party analytics counts toward the 100,000 figure is (unverified).

**CalOPPA (Bus. & Prof. Code 22575).** Requires a conspicuously posted privacy policy for commercial sites collecting PII from CA consumers, stating categories collected, categories of third parties shared with, how users review/change info, how changes are notified, effective date, and how the site responds to Do Not Track signals. Primary source fetch returned 403 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=22575); content here is from general knowledge, (unverified) this pass. Applies regardless of company size.

Most state laws are enforced by the state AG and have no private right of action (Nebraska stated: https://www.bassberry.com/news/huskers-du-privacy/).

**Global Privacy Control.** GPC is a user-enabled browser signal to opt out of sale/sharing (https://globalprivacycontrol.org/, 2026-11-24). California treats it as a valid opt-out request for covered businesses (https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa). Colorado's AG recognizes GPC as the valid universal opt-out mechanism (https://coag.gov/uoom/). Secondary reports say roughly a dozen states require honoring such signals (unverified list; New Jersey and Connecticut reported). Practical rule: if the site does not sell/share data or do targeted ads, a GPC signal has nothing to opt out of; still, honoring it (not loading ad trackers when GPC is on) is cheap.

**Cookies/consent norms.** US default is opt-out (notice plus a way to refuse sale/sharing), not EU-style opt-in; sensitive data (e.g., health, precise geolocation) often needs opt-in under state laws (general knowledge, (unverified) with a primary source). A self-assessment that infers health/fitness data could touch "sensitive data" rules in some states; collect it client-side only if possible.

**Privacy-friendly analytics.** Cookieless, first-party, aggregate analytics with no cross-site tracking and no sale/sharing minimize CCPA/state exposure and avoid needing a consent banner in the US (general reasoning; (unverified) with a regulator source). Disclose it in the privacy policy anyway; honor DNT/GPC where feasible; avoid IP/full-fingerprint retention.

### 2. Email (CAN-SPAM)
FTC guide (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business, 2026-11-24): accurate From/Reply-To; non-deceptive subject; identify ads; include a valid physical postal address; a clear opt-out; honor opt-outs within 10 business days; the opt-out mechanism must work 30 days after sending; you cannot outsource responsibility; penalty up to $53,088 per email. Valid address = current street address, a USPS-registered PO box, or a private mailbox registered with a commercial mail receiving agency. An overseas solo operator can therefore rent a US CMRA/virtual mailbox (service must register under USPS Form 1583 - (unverified)) or use a Turkish street address if privacy is acceptable. Double opt-in is not required by CAN-SPAM (general knowledge); it is recommended for consent proof, list hygiene, and ESP rules.

### 3. Children (COPPA)
"Child" = under 13; operators of sites directed to children, or with actual knowledge of collecting from a child, are covered (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/6501, 2026-11-24). GS-1811 applicants are adults (FBI age limits appear on fbijobs.gov), but candidates may be students. State: "Not directed to children under 13; we do not knowingly collect their data; email us for deletion." Do not use child-oriented imagery or content. No age gate needed for an adult-oriented site (general; (unverified) legal confirmation). (superseded 2026-11-24, see Addendum: audience now includes 17-year-olds)

### 4. Government-affiliation risk
- 18 U.S.C. 701: unlawful to make/sell/possess a badge, ID or insignia of the design prescribed by a federal agency, or a "colorable imitation"; up to 6 months (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/701).
- 18 U.S.C. 709: bars knowingly using "Federal Bureau of Investigation," "FBI," "Secret Service" or similar in a manner suggesting approval or endorsement without the Director's written permission; also covers US Marshals, DEA, and other named agencies; individuals up to 1 year; no general disclaimer exception in the text (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/709).
- 18 U.S.C. 712: false impression of federal origin in private debt collection or private investigation/detective services; up to 1 year (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/712). Not directly on point for a prep site, but shows the "false impression" approach.
- FTC Impersonation Rule, 16 CFR Part 461 (89 FR 15030, 2024-03-01; effective 2024-04-01): bans impersonating government or businesses and misrepresenting affiliation, including by implication; seals and insignia are examples, not a standalone ban. Covers only material misrepresentations in commerce. Civil penalties up to $53,088 per violation (https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/impersonation-government-businesses-rule; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/part-461; https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2024/02/new-impersonator-rule-gives-ftc-powerful-tool-protecting-consumers-businesses). Text of 461.2 itself not fetched in full (Federal Register fetch redirected).
- Agency practice: FBI has historically objected to unauthorized seal use (2010 Wikipedia dispute; eWeek https://www.eweek.com/enterprise-apps/fbi-threatens-to-sue-wikipedia-over-seal-use/, secondary). DOJ says requests to use a component's seal go to the component head (https://www.ojp.usdoj.gov/disclaim.htm). Current FBI-specific policy page was not found.
- Risk reducers: no seals, badges, agency logos, or look-alike colors/eagle/shield imagery; do not put "FBI," "DEA," "ATF," "DHS," or "Secret Service" in the site name, domain, or logo; descriptive/nominative references in body text are lower-risk but keep them factual; put a non-affiliation disclaimer in the footer, About page, and newsletter footer; avoid ".gov"-style wording ("official," "federal portal," "apply here"); link to USAJOBS and agency pages as the official sources. A disclaimer helps but does not cure a misleading overall impression (FTC "net impression" approach; general knowledge).

### 5. Content and IP
- 17 U.S.C. 105: works prepared by federal officers/employees as part of official duties are not copyrightable in the US; but works by contractors, third-party content embedded in them, and foreign copyright are not covered (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105, 2026-11-24). Public-domain status does not authorize use of seals/insignia (covered by 701/709) or trademarks.
- Official hiring tests: FBI applicants sign a nondisclosure form before the assessment; violating it can mean disqualification (https://fbijobs.gov/special-agents/application-and-evaluation-process; https://fbijobs.gov/sites/default/files/testing-overview.pdf). Do not reproduce, reconstruct, or solicit test questions from users. Publish only original practice items and general format descriptions from public agency guides. Commercial prep vendors hold copyright in their question banks (do not copy).
- Fair use is a case-by-case defense (four factors); copying whole question sets or lengthy passages is a poor fit (general knowledge). Quote briefly with attribution; summarize instead.
- Fitness/PFT content: include a "consult a physician before exercising; not medical advice; use at your own risk" disclaimer; do not promise passing results; avoid individualized prescription; (state-law enforceability of waivers varies - unverified).

### 6. Accessibility (ADA Title III)
DOJ's position: web content of businesses open to the public must be accessible, but it has no regulation specifying a technical standard for private businesses; WCAG is cited as useful guidance only (https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/, guidance dated 2022-03-18). Demand letters and lawsuits against small sites occur (general knowledge). Aim for WCAG 2.2 AA; publish an accessibility statement with a contact route. Another note covers details.

### 7. Operator in Turkey
- KVKK (Law 6698): no express territorial-scope clause; clearly applies to controllers established in Turkey; application to foreign data subjects is unsettled (https://www.linklaters.com/insights/data-protected/data-protected---turkey; https://www.kvkk.gov.tr/Icerik/7456/Purpose-and-Scope-of-The-Personal-Data-Protection-Law-No-6698; 2026-11-24). Conservative assumption: a Turkey-resident individual operator processing personal data (even of US users) may be a data controller under KVKK (disclosure/notice duties; VERBIS registration thresholds (unverified)). Get a Turkish lawyer's opinion.
- GDPR: Art. 3(2) applies to non-EU controllers only when offering goods/services to, or monitoring behavior of, people in the EU; EDPB guidelines turn on targeting indicators (language, currency, EU delivery), not mere accessibility (https://edpb.europa.eu/sites/edpb/files/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_3_2018_territorial_scope_en.pdf; the "mere accessibility" point is commonly read this way but I did not confirm the exact passage). English-only, USD, US-focused framing helps; do not market to the EU.
- Cross-border: Turkish Art. 9 as amended by Law 7499 (2024-03-02) allows transfers abroad via adequacy, safeguards such as standard contract (notify the Board within 5 business days of signing), or exceptional cases; Regulation of 2024-07-10 (https://cms.law/en/tur/legal-updates/turkiye-adopts-new-legal-framework-for-cross-border-transfer-of-personal-data). Whether sending Turkey-held data of non-Turkish persons to US hosts/ESPs triggers this is unsettled (unverified). Choose US-region hosting and an ESP with a DPA; collect only an email address.

### 8. Minimum contents
**Privacy policy:** operator identity and contact email; effective date; data collected (email, analytics, logs, any assessment answers) and whether stored server-side or only in-browser; purposes; third parties/processors (host, ESP, analytics); no sale/sharing statement; retention; user rights (access/delete/unsubscribe; state-law rights if applicable); DNT/GPC handling; children statement; international processing (operator in Turkey, data stored in US); security; change notice.
**Terms of use:** no affiliation/endorsement disclaimer; informational only, no guarantee of hiring or test results; fitness/health disclaimer; no-legal/career-advice limits; accuracy and "check official sources" statement; IP ownership and user license; acceptable use (no test-content submissions); limitation of liability and warranty disclaimer; governing law and venue choice (counsel); contact for takedowns/DMCA agent registration if user content is allowed (copyright.gov; not fetched).

## Addendum 2026-11-24: health data, minors, military marks

Scope: new readiness tool (height, weight, age, activity, self-test results; BMI computed in browser; nothing stored server-side; not sent to analytics), 17-year-old candidates, newsletter, Turkey-based operator. All accessed 2026-11-24. Information only, not legal advice.

### Findings
**A. Consumer health data (CHD) laws**
- Washington MHMDA (RCW 19.373): CHD = personal information linked or reasonably linkable to a consumer that reveals past/present/future physical or mental health, including "bodily functions, vital signs, symptoms, or measurements" and health data inferred from non-health data, including by algorithm. Weight, BMI and fitness are not named (https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/Law/RCW/RCW%20%2019%20%20TITLE/RCW%20%2019%20.373%20%20CHAPTER/RCW%20%2019%20.373%20.010.htm). A BMI/readiness result tied to a person is arguably CHD via "measurements" or inference; no regulator has said so for BMI (unverified).
- Size: no revenue floor. "Small business" (under 100,000 consumers, or under 25,000 plus under 50% revenue from CHD) only changed the start date (June 30, 2024) and still must comply with sections 4-9 (https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy). Private right of action via the WA Consumer Protection Act (same AG page). Reaches out-of-state operators that target WA consumers.
- "Collect" is defined as buy, rent, access, retain, receive, acquire, infer, derive or otherwise process in any way (RCW link above). Browser-only design is strong but not a clean exemption: the test is whether the operator receives, accesses or infers the data. Risk points: server logs, error reporting, analytics events carrying inputs/results, shareable result URLs with query strings, newsletter signup combined with results, third-party scripts that see the page. Whether purely client-side processing is "collecting" by the operator is untested (unverified; no regulator or court source found).
- Nevada SB 370 (NRS 603A.400-.550): CHD (603A.430) names "bodily functions, vital signs or symptoms" and inferred health data; weight/fitness not named, so coverage depends on use to identify health status. Regulated entity = does business in NV or targets NV consumers and decides purposes/means (603A.465). No size threshold; exemptions are by entity/data type (https://leg.state.nv.us/NRs/NRS-603A.html). AG enforcement, no private right (secondary summary, (unverified)).
- Connecticut (Public Act 23-56 / SB 3): CHD duties apply to any "person"/controller processing CT residents' CHD regardless of CTDPA thresholds (secondary: https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2023/07/The-Consumer-Health-Data-Amendments--to-the-Connecticut-Data-Privacy-Act). Statute text not fetched, (unverified). So the Connecticut 35k threshold in the Section 1 table does NOT limit the CHD provisions.
- Other states (CCPA/CPRA, Texas, Virginia etc.) treat health data as sensitive and often need opt-in consent; their size thresholds still apply (Section 1). Primary texts not fetched.

**B. FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (16 CFR 318, as amended; effective 2024-07-29 per secondary summary)**
- Covers non-HIPAA vendors of personal health records (PHR) and related entities. PHR vendor test: electronic record of identifiable health info with "technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources" (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2024/04/updated-ftc-health-breach-notification-rule-puts-new-provisions-place-protect-users-health-apps). A search summary of the 2024 rule says apps able to collect only directly from users are not covered (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0); the FTC blog does not address that case. Federal Register text not read (fetch redirects to an unverified host), so (unverified).
- "Breach of security" includes unauthorized disclosure, e.g. sharing health data with ad platforms (same FTC blog). A tool that sends BMI/inputs to analytics or ad pixels is the exposure.
- Browser-only effect: if the operator retains nothing and the tool takes manual entry only, PHR-vendor status is unlikely; adding wearable/API import or calendar integration would likely change that. Penalty up to $53,088 per violation (search summary of FTC guidance) vs $51,744 on the FTC basics page (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-breach-notification-rule-basics-business); see Contradictions.

**C. Claims substantiation and disclaimers**
- FTC: the same legal principles apply to marketing of any health-related product, including apps; health benefit claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence, generally randomized human testing; disclaimers do not cure a deceptive claim (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance, Dec 2022; first 100k characters read).
- Application: a "readiness score" or "you will pass" is an implied performance claim. Frame it as comparing self-entered numbers to published standards (arithmetic), not predicting outcomes. BMI is a screening ratio; some services also use tape body-fat methods, so a BMI "pass" cannot be promised.
- No FTC rule found mandating "consult a physician" wording for a general fitness tool ((unverified)); it is standard practice but not a defense to a misleading claim.

**D. Minors**
- COPPA: general-audience sites are covered only on actual knowledge of a user under 13; no duty to investigate age. A neutral age screen must not default to 13+ or hint at restrictions (https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions). Amended COPPA Rule: effective 2025-06-23, compliance 2026-04-22 (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule; dates from a search summary). The 17-18 audience is above 13, so COPPA risk is low; avoid content aimed at under-13s.
- 13-17: no federal law beyond COPPA yet; COPPA 2.0 passed the Senate in 2026, awaits House (https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/childrens-privacy-mid-year-update-key-legislative-developments-and-emerging-trends-for-businesses, 2026-08-04). State layer per that source: Connecticut minors provisions enforceable; Oregon HB 2008 (eff. 2026-01-01) bars selling minors' data and targeted ads to minors; New Hampshire HB 1460 (eff. 2027-01-01) bars selling minors' data; Maryland, Nebraska (actual-knowledge standard), Vermont ("reasonably likely" standard; effective 2027-01-01 or 2027-07-01, sources differ) and California design codes (CA partly enjoined; Ninth Circuit vacated part of the injunction 2026-03-12). Cutoff ages and thresholds not checked per statute (https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/nebraska-and-vermont-pass-age-appropriate-design-codes.html).
- Practical effect: these laws mostly bite on targeted ads, sale, profiling, and platforms. A no-ad, no-sale, no-profiling, client-side tool is low exposure, but knowledge of minors rises if the site asks age or markets to 17-year-olds. Server-side date-of-birth collection would create actual-knowledge records.
- Email: CAN-SPAM sets no age limit for commercial email (FTC guide, Section 2; general). The issue is state data law and ESP terms. Recommend 18+ newsletter signup with an unchecked "I am 18 or older" box. Whether an age screen is legally required for a 17-year-old audience is (unverified).

**E. Military marks**
- 10 U.S.C. 2260 lets each Secretary license marks they own/control and keep fees; it does not itself prohibit or define unauthorized use (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/2260). Coast Guard protection cited as 14 U.S.C. 639 (search summary only; fetch 404; (unverified)). 18 U.S.C. 701 covers badge/insignia designs (Section 4).
- Army ATLP: marks include seals, emblems, insignia, logos, mottos, wordmarks, names ("U.S. Army"); licence needed for commercial products and branding/advertising/marketing; marks must not imply endorsement; fair use is limited and case by case (https://www.army.mil/atlp). The site shows two different mailing addresses.
- Marine Corps: permission needed for commercial retail/advertising use of the Eagle, Globe and Anchor, seal, initials, name (https://www.hqmc.marines.mil/Agencies/Counsel-for-the-Commandant/Marine-Corps-Trademark-Licensing-Program/Commercial-Use, search snippet). A news article says a prominent non-endorsement disclaimer can replace permission in some uses (https://taskandpurpose.com/?p=184494, secondary, (unverified)).
- Air Force/Space Force: prior approval by the IP Management Program for any use of names/trademarks (https://www.trademark.af.mil/Air-Force, search summary). Navy: approval before using emblems/logos; do not copy logos from third-party sites (https://www.navy.mil/TRADEMARKS/Trademark-License/, search summary). Coast Guard: licence for commercial use of name/emblem; seal not to be reproduced (https://www.uscg.mil/Community/Trademark/, search summary, older FAQ). DoD archive page: service marks need "prior written permission"; no informational-use carve-out stated (https://www.webharvest.gov/congress114th/20161222171437/https://www.defense.gov/Media/Trademarks, Dec 2016 capture).
- Nominative/factual use: no service page read states a safe harbor for plain-text names such as "Army" or "Navy PFA". General nominative fair use (identify the thing, use only what is needed, no suggested sponsorship) is the likely frame but is untested against these programs (unverified). Seals, emblems, rank insignia and logos are the high-risk items; plain-text service names are lower risk.
